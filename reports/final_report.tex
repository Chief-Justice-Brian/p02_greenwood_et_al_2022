\begin{abstract}
This project reconstructs the country-year data and principal results of
Greenwood, Hanson, Shleifer, and S{\o}rensen's \textit{Predictable Financial
Crises} using public data. The paper's central predictor is the R-Zone: a
year in which three-year debt growth ranks in the historical top quintile and
real asset-price growth ranks in the historical top tercile. We reproduce the
assigned historical tables and event-history figures, validate the
reconstruction against 586 published benchmarks using documented tolerances,
and extend the predictors and R-Zone classifications through 2025. We then
examine bank fragility, missed crises, crisis dynamics, and the United States
during the 2023 banking stress. Overall, the replication recovers the paper's
key empirical patterns: sample frequencies, the cross-cell crisis gradient,
and the qualitative regression results. Remaining discrepancies arise
primarily from unavailable proprietary equity-price data, revisions to
public-data vintages, and differences in finite-sample inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Nature and Scope of the Replication}

The original paper asks whether financial crises are predictable from the
joint behavior of credit and asset prices. We reconstruct annual observations
for the paper's 42 countries beginning in 1950, using publicly available data
and matching the original definitions and sample construction wherever
possible. Business-sector R-Zones pair business debt-to-GDP growth with real
equity-price growth; household-sector R-Zones pair household debt-to-GDP
growth with real house-price growth. The historical forecast-origin sample
ends in 2012, allowing crisis outcomes to be observed one to four years
ahead, through 2016.

The project has three layers. First, we reconstruct the historical variables,
descriptive exhibits, and country-fixed-effects probability models. Second,
we hold the historical thresholds fixed and extend the underlying predictors
and principal exhibits through the latest usable observations. Third, we
examine whether aggregate bank-balance-sheet fragility and autoregressive
persistence add information beyond the original borrower-side R-Zone
indicators.

\section{Data Sources and Construction}

The crisis chronology, bank-equity crashes, bank failures, panics, and the
historical GDP series come from the Baron--Verner--Xiong replication data.
Credit is assembled from the IMF Global Debt Database, BIS Total Credit
Statistics, and the Jord\`a--Schularick--Taylor Macrohistory database. Real
equity prices use the IMF's former share-price series, JST, and OECD share
prices. Real house prices use BIS Residential Property Prices, OECD Analytical
House Prices, and JST. CPI and nominal GDP needed for real transformations come
from World Bank World Development Indicators with JST supplements.

Each three-year change is calculated within each source before series from
different sources are combined. This avoids constructing a growth rate across
incompatible levels.
Debt growth is measured as a three-year percentage-point change in debt to GDP;
real asset-price growth is 100 times a three-year log difference. Missing
values remain missing, and a future crisis outcome is defined only when the
entire relevant future window is covered. The historical pooled Q80 debt and
T66.7 price cutoffs are frozen for every later classification.

For the post-2016 crisis update, the outcome series extends BVX's own
definition forward, so ``crisis'' means one thing in every year of the panel:
a bank equity index decline of 30\% or more together with narrative evidence
of widespread bank failures or a banking panic. No published chronology
applies these criteria past 2016, so the extension is constructed here from
published datasets: the BVX replication data and the CRSP value-weighted bank
portfolio from the Ken French Data Library, which tracks the paper's own
United States bank equity series closely over their post-war overlap.

A narrative screen of the sample for 2017--2025 yields three candidate
episodes, and the underlying data determine each label mechanically
(Table~\ref{tab:crisis-screen}). The 2023 United States episode is genuinely
borderline: the broad value-weighted index stops short of the 30\% threshold
because the crash concentrated in regional banks while the largest
institutions rallied, even though large-cap bank benchmarks crossed the
threshold and the failure and run evidence is unprecedented in terms of
assets affected. BVX's own chronology contains a precedent in the other
direction---the United States in 1984 is counted as a crisis on narrative
evidence with a smaller aggregate equity decline---so we retain the
mechanical classification and report the sensitivity openly.

\subsection{Country-Fixed-Effects Prediction Models}

The regression exercise estimates linear probability models with country fixed
effects and Driscoll--Kraay covariance estimates. For each sector and horizon
$h$, the baseline specification is the paper's own Table 4 model, written in
compact notation:\footnote{$C^{h}_{i,t}$ equals one when a BVX crisis begins
in years $t+1$ through $t+h$; $D_{i,t}$ and $P_{i,t}$ are the sector's
high-debt-growth (top quintile) and high-price-growth (top tercile)
indicators; $R_{i,t} = D_{i,t} P_{i,t}$ is the R-Zone; $\alpha_i$ is a
country fixed effect. Coefficients are reported in percentage points.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:baseline}
C^{h}_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 D_{i,t} + \beta_2 P_{i,t} + \beta_3 R_{i,t}
+ \varepsilon_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
Across horizons, high debt growth and the R-Zone interaction carry the
economically important effects; price growth alone is less stable.
Coefficients are not expected to match exactly because R-Zone assignments are
sensitive to the unavailable proprietary equity histories.

\section{Extensions and Diagnostics}

\subsection{Bank-Balance-Sheet Fragility}
\label{sec:fragility}

The fragility extension asks whether bank balance sheets contain information
about crisis risk beyond the borrower-side R-Zone indicators, and whether
fragility changes the effect of an R-Zone. We add three measures one at a
time to the baseline specification in Equation~\eqref{eq:baseline}: high
noncore funding, high loans-to-deposits, and low bank capital (the leverage
measure is sign-flipped so that larger values indicate greater fragility),
together with each measure's interaction with the sector R-Zone:\footnote{$F_{i,t}$ flags one of the three
bank-fragility ratios at or above its historical 80th percentile; the
continuous robustness check below replaces $F_{i,t}$ with the standardized
ratio level $F^{z}_{i,t}$ in both terms.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fragility}
C^{h}_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 D_{i,t} + \beta_2 P_{i,t} + \beta_3 R_{i,t}
+ \gamma_1 F_{i,t} + \gamma_2 \left(R_{i,t} \times F_{i,t}\right)
+ \varepsilon_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
All comparisons use the same complete-case sample as the corresponding GHSS
specification. The three-year results point to a clear distinction between
\emph{fragility as a predictor} and \emph{fragility as an amplifier}. High
noncore funding and high loans-to-deposits are both positively and
significantly associated with crisis risk in the business and household
samples, and adding them raises within-model fit. The interaction terms, by
contrast, do not show a consistent pattern: only the leverage interactions
are statistically significant, and they have opposite signs across sectors.
The flag-based results therefore do not support a common amplification
mechanism.

Table~\ref{tab:fragility} makes this distinction visible. The clearest
result is for funding fragility. High noncore funding is associated with
14--15 percentage points higher crisis probability, while high
loans-to-deposits is associated with roughly 13 percentage points higher
probability in both sectors. Adding these measures raises the within-model
$R^2$ from 6.8 to 9.1--9.2 in the business sample and from 7.4 to 9.1--9.8
in the household sample. The leverage measures tell a different story. Their
standalone coefficients are not robustly positive, while the interaction
coefficients are large but opposite in sign across sectors: $-32.0$
percentage points for business leverage and $+40.5$ for household leverage.
That combination is difficult to interpret as evidence of a common economic
mechanism.

The continuous robustness check reaches the same conclusion for the funding
measures: funding fragility remains positively and significantly associated
with crisis risk, while the R-Zone interactions are indistinguishable from
zero. The evidence for an interaction is therefore weak and inconsistent,
whereas the evidence for funding fragility as an independent predictor is
much more robust. In other words, the results suggest that bank funding
fragility contains information about crisis risk beyond the R-Zone, but do
not convincingly show that it changes the effect of the R-Zone itself.
Adding funding fragility also produces a substantial increase in
within-model fit at the three- and four-year horizons.

Two further precommitted checks examine what kind of predictor fragility is.
Both are regression models: variants of the continuous specification that
add standardized noncore-funding terms to the fixed-effects linear
probability model in Equation~\eqref{eq:baseline}. First, when the current
noncore-funding level and its three-year change are included together as
competing regressors, the level remains positive and significant while the
change is not. Funding fragility therefore appears to matter because of the
level of reliance on noncore funding, rather than because that reliance has
recently increased. This contrasts with the paper's R-Zone predictors, which
are defined by three-year changes in credit and asset prices. Second, a
quadratic specification includes the standardized level together with its
square, so a meaningful squared coefficient would indicate that crisis risk
accelerates or flattens as fragility rises. The squared term is small and
statistically insignificant at every horizon, providing no evidence of a
nonlinear or threshold relationship. The results therefore support treating
funding fragility as a continuous measure of underlying vulnerability rather
than converting it into a binary warning signal.

\subsection{Crises Missed by the R-Zone}

Of 54 historical crisis onsets in the diagnostic sample, 26 were not
preceded by a business- or household-sector R-Zone in the prior three years.
Fragility data are available for 16 of these misses. Seven had prior noncore
funding above the 80th percentile, six had high loans-to-deposits, and two
had thin bank capital. Fragility therefore identifies a meaningful subset of
R-Zone misses, but not most of them. Missing JST banking coverage limits the
analysis further.

\subsection{Autoregressive Comparison}

The dynamic specification adds current and lagged crisis states and lagged
predictors to the same-sample GHSS model, implementing the local projection
the paper's footnote 10 describes:\footnote{$\mathit{Crisis}_{i,t}$ is the
contemporaneous BVX crisis state; the lagged terms are one-year
within-country lags.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dynamic}
C^{h}_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 D_{i,t} + \beta_2 P_{i,t} + \beta_3 R_{i,t}
+ \delta_0 \mathit{Crisis}_{i,t} + \delta_1 \mathit{Crisis}_{i,t-1}
+ \delta_2 D_{i,t-1} + \delta_3 P_{i,t-1} + \delta_4 R_{i,t-1}
+ \varepsilon_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
Within $R^2$ and in-sample AUC rise at
every horizon, most visibly at the one- and two-year horizons; crisis memory
sharpens the near view and adds little at the horizons the R-Zone is designed
for. Because the AUC values are in-sample, the exercise establishes
incremental fit rather than a definitive out-of-sample forecasting gain.

The paper's footnote 10 asserts, without a supporting table, that a local
projection with these dynamic terms gives ``qualitatively similar results.''
We pinned that phrase down before estimation: signs must match,
significance must survive at the same horizons, and the dynamic R-Zone
coefficient must sit within one standard error of its static value. All three
criteria hold in every sector-horizon cell, so the footnote's claim, which
the literature has so far taken on faith, verifies on our panel.

\subsection{Crisis Risk After Leaving the R-Zone}

The R-Zone can end when asset-price growth slows, potentially just as
financial conditions are becoming more dangerous. We test whether crisis
risk remains elevated for one, two, and three years after leaving the zone,
using mutually exclusive post-exit indicators added to the same-sample
baseline.

The results differ sharply by sector. Household post-exit years show
elevated crisis risk, while business post-exit years do not. But the
household result is not robust to excluding origins close to earlier crises.
A crisis itself crushes prices and ejects a country from the zone: roughly
two-fifths of household post-exit years occur within two years of a BVX
crisis, and the estimated effect is concentrated in the 2008--2011 crisis
wave. Once origins with a BVX crisis in the current or three prior years are
removed, the coefficients remain similar but are mostly no longer
statistically significant.

We therefore do not interpret post-exit status as a standalone warning
signal. The evidence suggests that leaving the R-Zone can coincide with
elevated crisis risk, particularly during crisis waves, but does not
establish that an exit itself provides additional advance warning.

\subsection{United States and the 2023 Banking Stress}

The United States was not in either aggregate R-Zone from 2019 through 2023.
This is a useful boundary case for the model: the R-Zone is designed to
identify broad borrower-side credit and asset-price booms, not concentrated
vulnerabilities within the banking system. The 2023 banking stress instead
centered on duration losses, reliance on uninsured deposits, funding
fragility, and bank runs at particular institutions. The aggregate
predictors therefore did not signal the episode, even though severe
vulnerabilities were building within parts of the banking system.

Our extended chronology gives the outcome side of the same verdict.
Applying BVX's criteria to the pulled data records no United States crisis
onset in 2023: the broad value-weighted bank index fell short of the 30\%
decline criterion, and the episode enters the panel as a documented
borderline call (Table~\ref{tab:crisis-screen}). Under the panel's own
bookkeeping, then, 2023 is a quiet signal followed by no labeled crisis, so
the monitor is not scored as having missed anything. The tension is
interpretive rather than mechanical: a binary label cannot distinguish the
absence of a fire from a fire that was put out quickly.

The distinction matters for interpretation. A failure to trigger the R-Zone
does not imply that the banking system was broadly healthy; it means that
the particular combination of credit growth and asset-price growth used by
the R-Zone was not unusually high. The JST banking ratios end in 2020 in
the latest public release, so the aggregate fragility gauge cannot be
computed for 2021--2023 from any public source.

\section{Assessment: Successes, Challenges, and Lessons}

\subsection*{Where the Replication Succeeded}

The project produces an end-to-end public-data reconstruction with a common
country-year key, documented source priorities, reproducible
transformations, and automated validation. It closely recovers the paper's
R-Zone frequencies, preserves the joint debt--price crisis gradient, and
produces regression results with the same broad economic interpretation. All
586 transcribed historical benchmarks are audited under paper-scaled
tolerances: 545 pass, and the 41 misses are concentrated in sparse Table 3
cells and Table 4 inference and fit statistics.

\subsection*{Where the Project Faced Challenges}

The main limitation is data equivalence. The paper relies in part on
proprietary Global Financial Data and Bloomberg equity histories that are
not publicly available. We therefore use IMF, JST, and OECD alternatives.
These reproduce the historical thresholds closely, but differences in
volatility and country coverage can change individual R-Zone assignments.
Public data have also been revised since the original publication, and long
historical series require careful source-by-source construction. On
inference, the project implements Driscoll--Kraay covariance estimates but
not the paper's additional Kiefer--Vogelsang finite-sample correction.

The modern extension has an important limitation: no confirmed post-2016
crisis onset occurs within the 42-country sample under the paper's criteria.
The additional years therefore show how the warning states evolved after
publication, but cannot yet provide a meaningful out-of-sample test of
crisis prediction. Bank-fragility data are also more limited in coverage and
capture aggregate banking conditions rather than institution-specific
vulnerabilities, as in Continental Illinois or Silicon Valley Bank.

\subsection*{Overall Conclusion}

The public reconstruction supports the paper's central qualitative result:
rapid debt growth is most concerning when accompanied by rapid real
asset-price growth. The R-Zone is therefore useful as a macro-financial
warning state, but it is not a complete crisis model.

The extensions point to two complementary sources of information. Crisis
memory improves near-term prediction, while bank funding fragility provides
an additional, continuous signal over the three- to four-year horizon. The
evidence does not show that fragility systematically amplifies the R-Zone;
rather, it appears to predict crisis risk independently. Missed crises and
the 2023 United States banking stress further show the limits of aggregate
country-level boom indicators when vulnerabilities arise within the banking
system rather than through a broad credit and asset-price boom.

The updated data do not overturn the historical relationship. At the same
time, the absence of a confirmed post-2016 systemic crisis onset in the
sample means that the modern extension cannot yet provide a strong
out-of-sample validation of the R-Zone. Its strongest contribution is
instead to show how the original warning state behaves in later data and
where complementary banking-system indicators become important.
